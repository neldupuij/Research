==============================================================
  SPEAKER NOTES — Volatility Spillovers Among European Financial Institutions
  Target: ~18–19 minutes  |  22 slides
==============================================================

──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
SLIDE 1 — Title  [~30s]
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Good [morning/afternoon] everyone. Today I will present our work on volatility
spillovers and connectedness across major European financial institutions.
We combine three complementary approaches — VAR-based connectedness, DCC-GARCH,
and correlation-network methods — to study how risk propagates across banks,
insurers, and diversified financials in Europe.

──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
SLIDE 2 — Motivation  [~1min 30s]
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Why does this matter? Over the past decades, financial integration has created
denser networks through which local shocks can quickly become systemic events.
The GFC, the European sovereign debt crisis, and COVID-19 are vivid reminders
of this. In Europe specifically, the sovereign-bank nexus means that sovereign
stress can weaken domestic banks and spill over internationally through
cross-border linkages.

This creates a complicated problem. For policymakers: is a troubled institution
"Too Big to Fail", "Too Many to Fail", or "Too Interconnected to Fail"?
For investors: are there real diversification opportunities during systemic stress?

To answer these questions, we need directional, ordering-invariant measures of
connectedness — measures that can capture regime-dependent dynamics from the
individual bank level all the way up to the country level.

Our three main questions are: How dense and state-dependent is connectedness?
Which institutions act as persistent transmitters or receivers? And do our three
methodologies deliver consistent systemic risk signals?

──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
SLIDE 3 — Literature  [~1min]
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
The paper sits at the intersection of three strands of literature.

First, the VAR-DY connectedness framework, starting from Diebold and Yilmaz
(2009, 2012), which uses generalized forecast error variance decompositions to
extract directed spillover networks. A very recent extension by Buchwalter,
Diebold and Yilmaz (2025) introduces clustered identification — we benchmark
our results against this line of work.

Second, multivariate GARCH models — and in particular the DCC model of Engle
(2002), which is tractable even in large systems and delivers time-varying
correlations we can compare directly with our VAR-based connectedness.

Third, correlation networks and MSTs, following Mantegna (1999), which offer a
model-free view of market topology and clustering.

The key gap: no prior study jointly applies the full DY framework to European
financial institutions while benchmarking DCC and comparing with MST networks
across the full institutional cross-section.

──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
SLIDE 4 — Data  [~1min]
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Our dataset covers 63 European financial institutions — banks, insurers, and
diversified financials — from 18 countries, over the period 2010 to 2026.

Daily prices come from Yahoo Finance. We construct Friday-to-Friday weekly
log-returns and standardize each series by its full-sample mean and standard
deviation. This standardization removes level differences and allows us to focus
on second-moment dynamics.

Institutions are included if they have data from January 2010, at most 20%
missing weekly observations, no prolonged suspensions, and if their country has
at least 3 surviving institutions. The result is a balanced panel of 682 weekly
observations per series.

──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
SLIDE 5a — Methodology: VAR-DY  [~1min 30s]
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Our main methodology is the Diebold-Yilmaz framework. The workflow is:
standardized returns → rolling VAR(p) → VMA(∞) → generalized FEVD →
connectedness measures.

The key object is the generalized FEVD entry θ̃ᵍᵢⱼ(H), which measures the share
of the H-step-ahead forecast error variance of institution i that is attributable
to shocks from institution j. Because we use the generalized identification of
Koop, Pesaran and Shin, these shares are invariant to variable ordering.

From these we construct three measures:
— The Total Connectedness Index (TCI): the average off-diagonal share, summarizing
  system-wide interconnectedness.
— Directional FROM and TO: how much each institution receives from, or transmits
  to, the rest of the system.
— Net spillover: TO minus FROM — positive means net transmitter, negative means
  net receiver.

Our baseline uses p=1, a rolling window of 156 weeks (≈3 years), a step of 4
weeks, and a forecast horizon H=10 weeks.

──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
SLIDE 5b — Methodology: VAR-DY (cont.)  [~30s]
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
For robustness, we vary the VAR order (p=1 vs p=2), the window length
(W = 104, 156, or 208 weeks), and the forecast horizon (H = 4 to 12 weeks).
All key results survive these variations — I will return to this at the end.

──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
SLIDE 6 — Benchmarks: DCC-GARCH & MST  [~1min]
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
We benchmark the VAR-DY results against two model-free approaches.

For DCC-GARCH: we first fit a univariate GARCH(1,1) to each series, then model
the dynamic correlations via the DCC updating equation of Engle (2002). This
gives us a time-varying correlation matrix Rₜ at each point. In each rolling
window, we compare the mean absolute DCC correlation with the VAR-DY TCI.

For the MST: we build a correlation-based distance matrix using the Mantegna
transformation dᵢⱼ = √(2(1−ρ̂ᵢⱼ)), then extract the minimum spanning tree —
the minimal connected subgraph spanning all 63 nodes. The MST provides a
model-free backbone of the strongest pairwise dependencies in the system.

──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
SLIDE 7a — Results: TCI Dynamics  [~1min 30s]
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Let's move to the results. The first and perhaps most striking finding is how
high system-wide connectedness is — and how it evolves.

The TCI remains in the upper 80s even in tranquil periods. This means that at
any point in time, roughly 85–90% of each institution's forecast error variance
is driven by shocks from other institutions — not by idiosyncratic factors.
The system is already tightly integrated before any crisis hits.

When COVID-19 arrives in early 2020, the TCI spikes sharply to around 95%,
compressing idiosyncratic components almost entirely.

But the most important observation is what happens after: TCI does not return to
its pre-2019 level. It stabilizes structurally above the pre-COVID norm.
This is not just a temporary shock — it is a lasting structural shift in the
topology of the European financial network.

──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
SLIDE 7b — Results: Frequency Decomposition  [~1min]
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
To understand the nature of this structural shift, we decompose the TCI across
frequency bands following Baruník and Křehlík (2018).

In calm periods, short-run spillovers — cycles of 2 to 4 weeks — dominate.
These reflect transient market co-movement and sentiment contagion.

During and after COVID, the long-run band — cycles exceeding 8 weeks — expands
from roughly 25% to 45% of total TCI, and stays elevated. This tells us the
post-COVID ratcheting of connectedness is driven by structural deepening of
financial integration, not just transient panic.

At the institutional level, UBS is the dominant long-run structural transmitter,
yet it does not appear in the top-5 short-run emitters. This frequency
heterogeneity is invisible if you only look at aggregate TCI.

──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
SLIDE 8 — Results: FROM & TO Rankings  [~1min]
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
While the TCI tells us about the system as a whole, the directional measures
reveal the heterogeneous roles of individual institutions.

Looking at the FROM rankings — the top receivers of spillovers — we consistently
find large cross-border banks: BNP Paribas, Société Générale, ING Group,
Santander, and UBS.

Looking at the TO rankings — the top transmitters — we find a somewhat different
set: Société Générale, Bankinter, Raiffeisen, Skandinaviska Enskilda Banken,
and KBC Group.

The key message: even in a highly connected system, individual roles differ
substantially. Some institutions primarily absorb shocks, others primarily
generate them — and a few do both.

──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
SLIDE 9 — Results: Net Spillovers  [~1min]
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
The net spillover picture is equally instructive. A positive net value means the
institution transmits more volatility than it receives — a net emitter.

We find that banks from peripheral or smaller markets tend to be persistent net
transmitters: their volatility propagates outward to the rest of the system.
Large Western European banks tend to be structural net receivers: they absorb
more volatility than they emit.

And crucially, several large cross-border institutions play a dual role —
appearing simultaneously among the top receivers and top emitters. They act
as both amplifiers and targets of systemic volatility.

The macroprudential implication is direct: systemic risk is not simply a function
of size. Peripheral net transmitters deserve closer supervisory attention, and the
dual-role institutions complicate any simple "Too Big to Fail" narrative.

──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
SLIDE 10 — Results: DCC Dynamics  [~45s]
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Turning to the DCC-GARCH benchmark: the mean absolute correlation across all
institution pairs — what we call ρ̄ₜ — confirms the picture from the VAR-DY TCI.

In calm periods, ρ̄ₜ is around 0.30–0.35. This is not zero: European financial
equities are structurally co-dependent even in tranquil times.
During COVID, ρ̄ₜ jumps above 0.50 and stays elevated — closely matching
the timing of the VAR-DY TCI surge.

The same stable core of institutions emerges as DCC hubs: BNP Paribas, Société
Générale, ING, Intesa Sanpaolo, KBC, Santander, and UBS.

──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
SLIDE 11 — Results: DY vs DCC  [~45s]
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
How closely do the two approaches track each other? Very closely.

The Pearson correlation between rolling TCI and mean DCC is r = 0.94, with
R² = 0.88. The OLS line fits the time series almost perfectly. Both methods
spike simultaneously during COVID and remain elevated together.

One additional finding: DCC leads TCI by approximately one rolling window.
This means the fast-moving covariance structure contains early-warning
information about upcoming shifts in variance-decomposition connectedness.

──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
SLIDE 11b — Results: Connectedness Regimes  [~1min]
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
To go beyond descriptive analysis of the TCI, we apply k-means clustering
directly to the rolling GFEVD feature vectors — with no pre-labelling of
crisis episodes.

For each of the 132 rolling windows, we compute a 5-dimensional feature vector
capturing: TCI, cross-sectional dispersion and skewness of net spillovers,
the maximum net transmitter, and top-5 concentration.

The silhouette criterion selects K=2 regimes. A calm regime: TCI around 88%,
high dispersion, positive skew — one dominant transmitter is identifiable.
And a crisis regime: TCI around 94%, lower dispersion, negative skew.

The key insight is that during crises, the network homogenizes. Shocks spread
more uniformly — there is no single dominant transmitter driving the system.
This structural feature of the crisis regime is completely invisible to
aggregate TCI analysis alone, and has direct implications for stress-test design.

──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
SLIDE 12 — Results: Cross-Method Centrality  [~45s]
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
A natural question is whether the systemic risk rankings are robust across
methods. The answer is a strong yes.

On a comparable strength basis, the Spearman rank correlation between VAR-DY
time-averaged TO strength and simple correlation-network strength is ρ = 0.97,
statistically significant across all 63 institutions. For the MST weighted
degree, it is ρ = 0.70.

This near-perfect alignment means that even a model-free, computationally simple
correlation ranking recovers the same hierarchy of systemic importance as the
more sophisticated GFEVD framework. Our risk hub identification is robust.

──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
SLIDES 12b, 12c, 12d — Networks Across Regimes  [~1min total]
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
These three slides show the VAR-DY, correlation, and MST networks side by side
across the three sub-periods: calm, crisis, and post-crisis.

In all three methods, the pattern is consistent: the network densifies sharply
during the crisis period, hub degrees increase, and the dominance of single
transmitters attenuates — confirming the homogenization result we saw from the
k-means analysis. Post-crisis, the network remains structurally more connected
than in the pre-COVID period.

──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
SLIDE 13 — Results: Country-Level TCI Network  [~1min]
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
We now zoom out to the country level. For each country c, we compute a
within-country TCI — the average share of forecast error variance attributable
to shocks from other domestic institutions. We then compute the correlation
between these country-level TCI series and build a network.

Five geographical communities emerge via Louvain detection.
Group 1: the core DE/ES pair. Group 2: a broader core Western group including
CH, NL, AT, FR, UK and RO. Group 3: a secondary cluster — Ireland, Poland,
Denmark, Norway. Group 4: Italy and Sweden, more idiosyncratic. And Group 5:
Greece, almost entirely isolated from the rest.

This heterogeneity matters for portfolio construction. Countries whose domestic
connectedness moves less synchronously — like Greece and Sweden — offer natural
geographic diversification.

──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
SLIDE 13b — Results: Between-Country Spillovers  [~1min]
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Finally, we measure direct cross-border spillovers using the formula on screen:
we average the GFEVD entries across all institution pairs spanning two countries,
and do this for each rolling window.

The 18×18 directional spillover matrix shows clear asymmetry — rows with
uniformly high values identify the main exporting national systems.
UK, France, Germany, and Spain consistently appear as the strongest net
exporters of volatility across European borders.

The directed network on the right makes this visual: the large blue node at
the center is France — the dominant net receiver — while Romania and Belgium
appear as net emitters. The arrow directions confirm the genuine directionality
that a symmetric correlation approach would miss entirely.

──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
SLIDE 14 — Robustness  [~45s]
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
All main results are robust along three dimensions.

VAR order: p=1 and p=2 give virtually identical TCI dynamics and directional
rankings. AIC/BIC consistently prefer p=1.

Forecast horizon: varying H from 4 to 12 weeks shifts the TCI level slightly
at shorter horizons, but rankings and time-variation patterns are preserved.

Rolling window: varying W between 104 and 208 weeks preserves the COVID spike,
the structural elevation, and the institutional rankings. Shorter windows
amplify fluctuations; longer windows smooth them.

And across all specifications, the near-linear DY–DCC association holds with
r > 0.90.

──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
SLIDE 15 — Conclusion  [~1min 30s]
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Let me conclude with four main takeaways.

First: European financial markets are structurally highly connected —
TCI stays above 85% even in calm periods and surges to 95% during COVID,
with a lasting post-COVID structural ratchet.

Second: connectedness is not homogeneous. Large cross-border banks dominate as
dual receivers and emitters, while peripheral-market banks are persistent net
transmitters — the system is not simply "Too Big to Fail."

Third: the post-COVID shift is structural and long-run. The frequency
decomposition shows the long-run band nearly doubling its share of total TCI —
this is durable rewiring, not transient panic.

Fourth: systemic risk rankings are robust across methods. VAR-DY, DCC-GARCH,
and MST centrality all point to the same institutions as the key risk hubs.

For future work, we plan to extend to tail-risk connectedness via quantile
approaches, test whether connectedness predicts future systemic volatility,
and incorporate a portfolio backtesting dimension.

Thank you — I am happy to take questions.
